Honda CB750 Chopper

Applications: Illustrator, Photoshop

A design proposal for SCAD’s electric Honda CB750 chopper, told through three posters about what rebellion on two wheels meant in 1970, means today, and could mean in 2060.

Brief

SCAD is building an electric motorcycle from scratch, and I was asked to develop the design proposal for a chopper based on the Honda CB750. Before deciding what this new bike should look like, I needed to understand what a chopper has always stood for.

Three Rebellions. A chopper is, at its core, an expression of resistance and freedom. So instead of designing one look, I picked three moments in time that I felt represented rebellion most strongly, each with a real motorcycle culture at its center.

1970, Bōsōzoku. Japanese youth gangs who mixed American chopper culture with British café racers and rebelled against a society built on conformity. This is what the chopper used to represent.

2020, Deus Ex Machina. A custom motorcycle culture that blends vintage design with contemporary riding, keeping the spirit of rebellion alive as a way of life. This is what it represents today.

2060, Cyberpunk. My imagination of where it goes next. Riders pushing back against total control through futuristic technology, aggressive mechanical design, and dystopian cities.

Design

Each poster speaks the language of its own era, but all three share an underlying structure so they read as a set. The type is always yellow. The same block sits in the top left with “Honda CB750,” “Chopper,” a one-line description of the culture, and the year. And each poster shows only one part of the motorcycle, framed with simple shapes, a nod to the old ads that sold choppers piece by piece.

1970, Bōsōzoku. The rear wheel and exhaust sit behind rows of red wave patterns, a traditional Japanese motif. Running down the center is 夜露死苦, “yoroshiku,” a greeting that bōsōzoku gangs wrote with kanji chosen for their menacing meanings, like night, dew, death, and suffering. It was a perfect example of how they turned something ordinary into something defiant.

2020, Deus Ex Machina. The front fork, headlight, and wheel are rendered in a high-contrast blue over a diamond plate pattern, the industrial texture of the modern custom garage. “MACHINA” runs the full height of the poster in condensed Oswald, cutting through the wheel.

2060, Cyberpunk. The engine, the heart of the bike, takes over the frame in acid pink linework. Bold diagonal stripes cut across the background, and “Matrices” repeats in Orbitron, layered in solid and outlined versions that echo the glitchy, data-driven world I imagined riders rebelling against.

Process

I built a guideline for each era before designing its poster. Bōsōzoku uses Acumin with red, black, and gold, along with Japanese cloud and wave patterns. Deus Ex Machina uses the condensed Oswald, steel blues, and diamond plate and bold graphic patterns. Cyberpunk uses the squared, technical Orbitron with acid yellow and circuit-like forms.

Research

What a Chopper Is. Choppers grew out of the bobber. After World War II, returning servicemen stripped their bikes of everything they didn’t need, like fenders, front brakes, and turn signals, to make them lighter and faster for dirt-track racing. Choppers took that idea much further. A bobber is minimalist, a chopper is expressive, and the one rule a true chopper can’t break is that the frame neck is cut and re-welded to change its geometry.

The earliest choppers were built from cheap surplus Harley-Davidsons. When Japanese manufacturers began offering larger engines in the late 1960s, builders quickly turned to the Honda 750-4. Over time choppers stopped chasing performance and became about the look. Candy-flake paint, tall ape hangers, extended forks, and hardtail frames, popularized by films like Easy Rider and builders like Arlen Ness. Form over function.

Reference images from my research.

Why the CB750. The CB750 makes a chopper twice as rebellious. Builders took a Japanese superbike, made by a company that wasn’t the standard Harley-Davidson, and rejected everything it was designed to be. A longer rake, taller bars, a ride that is uncomfortable on purpose. The freedom is in the rejection.

Old CB750 chopper ads showed this too. They focused on individual parts rather than the whole motorcycle, because for a builder the parts are where the personality lives. That idea became the backbone of the posters.

Takeaway

The chopper has never been just a motorcycle. It is a statement about refusing what you are handed. By tracing that same rebellion across three eras, the posters give SCAD’s electric CB750 a history to build from and a reason to exist beyond its parts.
