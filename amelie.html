Amélie

RoleDesigner Timeline5 Weeks CourseTypographic Communication, GDVX-501 SchoolSCAD Atlanta

A typographic book and motion piece interpreting the 2001 film Amélie, exploring how ordinary life, when seen through the right eyes, becomes something quietly extraordinary. Designed and physically produced over five weeks.

The Film

Directed by Jean-Pierre Jeunet and released in 2001, Amélie follows Amélie Poulain, a shy young woman working at a Parisian café who discovers her natural ability to quietly bring joy to the people around her, all while navigating her own loneliness and the possibility of love. The film went on to become one of the highest-grossing foreign-language films of its time.

Its tone, quirky, warm, nostalgic, bright, made it an ideal subject for typographic translation: a film where small, specific details carry enormous emotional weight.

Concepts

Concept 1, Little Things explored Amélie's world through homemade, nostalgic feeling. Almost every character in the film is introduced through small, specific likes and dislikes rather than dramatic events. The concept used a leather-bound A5 format with folds, pop-outs, and die cuts. Typefaces: Parisine (designed by Jean François Porchez, used in the Paris Metro) and Archer.

Concept 2, Loneliness Hurts leaned into Amélie's isolation, raised by a distant father, losing her mother early, never developing real human connection. The format was a loose A4 bound with envelopes, treating the book as something fragile and archival rather than resolved. Typefaces: Garamond and Helvetica (deliberately cold alongside the humanist Garamond).

Concept 3, Romance of Reality (Chosen) grounded the project in the film's core observation: nothing that happens to Amélie is extraordinary, yet her relationship with the world makes it feel enviable. The book was printed tabloid (11 x 16"), hard cover, with die cuts and embossing. Typefaces: Baskerville and Avenir, one structured and classical, the other clear and neutral, together suggesting a world that is grounded yet open.

Structure

The book is organized around three key scenes from the film, each treated as its own chapter with a distinct typographic mood. Scene one follows Amélie discovering a childhood time capsule and returning it anonymously. Scene two traces her fascination with a stranger's discarded photobooth collection. Scene three brings the emotional resolution, her final choice to be seen rather than disappear.

Each spread built from that scene's emotional register: quiet and searching in scene one, curious and layered in scene two, open and present in scene three. The die cuts and embossing were used at structurally meaningful moments rather than decoratively.

Physical Production

Every aspect of production was handled independently, from layout to print to hand binding. The tabloid format required custom trimming and a hard cover assembled by hand. Die cuts were applied per spread, and the embossing was done using a heat tool to create tactile relief on the cover and selected interior pages.

Motion

The motion piece was conceived as a stop-motion typographic animation, cut-up letters arranged by hand on paper, filmed frame by frame with a warm yellow tint matching the film's palette. The audio uses the opening narration of Amélie, a sequence that introduces a gnat, a surprising restaurant incident, a funeral, and finally the birth of Amélie Poulain herself.

Each scene is its own sheet of paper. When the narrator moves to the next scene, the paper is crumpled, torn, or ripped away. The final scene, Amélie's birth, leaves the word "Amélie" alone in the center of frame, slowly zooming in to hold on the name as the title card.

Takeaway

Amélie works as a subject precisely because it asks for nothing grand. The challenge was building a visual system that could carry the same weight as the film, a world where small choices feel significant and ordinary moments become worth looking at twice. Baskerville and Avenir together achieved that balance: structural enough to feel real, open enough to feel felt.
